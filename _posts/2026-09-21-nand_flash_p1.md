# Reuse NAND Flash from SSD, Part 1

![image](https://github.com/youheng7185/youheng7185.github.io/blob/main/uploads/nand_p1/pic1.jpg?raw=true)

I am writing this to record down the process of reusing NAND flash from my failed SSD. That SSD was Sandisk 480GB SSD Plus using SATA protocol to communicate with the computer. Previously, it was being used as a Ubuntu boot disk for me to compile android custom rom. So after massive ccache and enormous amount of read writes, it failed right after the warranty expires.

And recently the price of SSD is really high due to AI datacenter consumptions, and my friend told me that he couldn't afford some larger volume SSD due to this. This made me thinking whether I can reuse these NAND flash from my failed SSD. NAND flash condition is not just classifies into perfectly fine or totally unusable. They are always in the middle, so there is health percentage to monitor from CrystalDisk info. I thought that my failed SSD just means the controller found too many uncorrectable ECC errors and just gave up to let me read and write anymore. That doesn't means my NAND flash is totally unusable anymore.

Here's the image of the SSD after I removed the NAND flash. The basic concept of SSD or any kind of hard disk is quite simple, the host, usually computers send command to read, write and erase logical sectors. The SSD, HDD, emmc, SD cards will satisfy the requirment of the computer. There is Flash Translation Layer to translate the logical sectors to the physical sectors on our nand flash. So the first sector that you write when creating a partition is not actually the first logical sectors on the real NAND flash.

To briefly explain what's inside the SSD, by taking mine as an example, the middle QFN chip is the flash controller, it has SATA slave controller, some kind of microcontroller and the NAND flash controller to talk to NAND. The FTL operations, ECC, garbage collection are all done by this ASIC. On the back of the board, we can see a QFN chip with some inductors around. This is surely DCDC converters, based on my memory, it receives 5V, generates 3.3V, 1.8V and 1.0V. 3.3V is for powering up the NAND, 1.8V is the VDDIO for NAND and flash controller, 1.0V is the VDDCore for the flash controller. Usually there is a pin header where you can short them to enter manufacturing mode, but SanDisk manufacturing tools are not disclosed to the public, so there's nothing to play around.

Now we talk about reversing the NAND flash, after I removed all of them with heat gun and clean the pads, I counted the number of pads on the flash. After a few searches on the internet, its BGA132, and the basic NAND flash specs are available online. Currently, there is two NAND standard, called ONFI and Toggle. Firstly I guessed this NAND is following the ONFI standard as Western Digital is one of the contributor to ONFI. But later on I found out its following Toggle standard. But both of the standard is having the same physical layout package for the NAND ic.

Then, I turn to the page of ONFI 3.2 because it shows the pins for that package that I was having. By using a multimeter in resistance mode, I can verify whether the pins is actually connected to something. After a few rounds of probing, I can confirm my NAND ic has two chip select and only used the pins with _0 conventions. That means BGA132 package allowing manufacturers to put two NAND flash die in a single package. But I don't know whether the phrase 'NAND flash die' is correct, because I having two CE (chip select) literally means there is two die that shares the same DQ0 to DQ7 pins.

Later on, I plugged in the scrapped SSD board to a SATA enclosure. Then, I measured the VCCQ and VCC voltage, its 1.8V and 3.3V, following ONFI standards also, good. Next up, I opened up Kicad and draw a breakout board so I could use that NAND with my microcontroller or FPGA. You may asked, I could just get those Silicon Motion flash controller with SATA or NVME interface to reuse the NAND flash, but I would like to reimplement those as learning activities to kill time.

![image](https://github.com/youheng7185/youheng7185.github.io/blob/main/uploads/nand_p1/pcb_my_ver.jpg?raw=true)

The board is quite simple, it has the BGA132 footprint, four logic converters that bidirectionally converts 1.8v and 3.3v (currently I do not have any MCU or FPGA with 1.8V logic around, so I need 3.3V logic). This NAND is using 1.8v logic as the VCCQ is 1.8V. The top SOT-2-3 ic was a 3.3V and 1.8V LDO to generate the required voltage for NAND. On the back, I put 0.1uF capacitor on each VCC and VCCQ pins. Also a RC circuit to pull up WP_N pins after a short delay.
